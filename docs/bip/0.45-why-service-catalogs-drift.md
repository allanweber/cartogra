## Blog Post (~700 words)

### Why Your Service Catalog Is Already Lying to You

Six months after your team launches a microservice platform, the wiki page titled "Our Services" has seventeen entries. Your production environment has forty-three services. Nobody knows when the gap opened. Nobody will admit they stopped updating it.

This is not a discipline problem. It is an architecture problem.

**The root cause: manual = drift**

A service catalog is a claim about a system. The system changes constantly — new services deployed, old ones decommissioned, teams reorganised, repositories renamed. Every one of those changes creates an opportunity for the catalog to fall behind.

The naive fix is process. A runbook entry: "When you deploy a new service, update the wiki." It works for about two sprints. Then an engineer is under deadline pressure. Then another one joins the team and doesn't know about the wiki. Then the wiki moves to Confluence and the migration skips half the entries.

The real problem is that you are asking humans to maintain a secondary representation of a truth that already exists in your infrastructure. The truth is in Kubernetes. It is in GitHub. It is in your OpenTelemetry traces. Your catalog is a hand-copied version of that truth, and hand-copied things drift.

**The cost of drift**

A stale catalog is worse than no catalog. With no catalog, engineers know they are flying blind and adapt. With a stale catalog, engineers make decisions based on wrong information — and they trust it because it looks official.

Drift means:
- Incident responders waste time during an outage checking whether a service still exists
- New engineers onboard to a map of a city that no longer resembles the actual city
- Blast radius analysis is wrong because missing services are missing from the graph
- Dependency edges that no longer exist are treated as current, causing false alerts

**What auto-discovery changes**

The alternative is to derive the catalog from the sources of truth rather than copying from them. When a new repository is pushed to GitHub, your system detects it. When a new workload appears in Kubernetes, your system notices. When OpenTelemetry traces show a service calling another service that has no declared dependency, your system flags it.

The catalog stops being a document that humans maintain. It becomes an index over real infrastructure signals — continuously updated, queryable, and trustworthy.

This is the core insight behind Cartogra. Every service in the registry was either detected automatically (via SCM scan or K8s watch) or declared explicitly via API. There is no wiki. There is no spreadsheet. The catalog is the live state, and any deviation from declared intent surfaces as a detectable drift.

**Temporal history**

Auto-discovery solves the present. Temporal history solves the past.

Knowing that a service exists today is useful. Knowing that it existed six months ago, was renamed, had its team ownership transferred, and once had a dependency on a now-decommissioned auth service — that is what incident retrospectives are actually asking for.

Cartogra tracks every change to a service as an immutable history entry. You can query what the dependency graph looked like on any date. You can understand which services existed at the time of a historical incident. You can audit ownership changes for compliance purposes.

**The takeaway**

If your service catalog requires a human to remember to update it, it will drift. The question is not whether — it is when and by how much. The fix is not a better process for updating the wiki. The fix is a system that does not require the update, because it reads directly from the sources of truth your infrastructure already produces.

Start with auto-discovery. Add temporal history. Surface drift as a first-class observable event. That is the sequence that makes a catalog trustworthy.

## LinkedIn (~350 words)

**Why your service catalog drifts — and what to do about it**

Six months after launching a microservice platform, most teams have the same problem: the catalog says one thing, production says another. The gap between "declared" and "real" is often invisible until an incident exposes it.

This is not a discipline problem. It is an architecture problem.

Manual catalogs ask engineers to maintain a secondary representation of infrastructure truth. That truth already exists — in Kubernetes, in GitHub repositories, in OpenTelemetry traces. When humans are the synchronisation mechanism between live infrastructure and a catalog document, drift is inevitable.

The naive fix is process enforcement: "update the wiki when you deploy." This works for a sprint or two. Then deadlines arrive, engineers context-switch, new joiners aren't briefed, and migrations lose entries. Every manual step is a potential desync.

The deeper fix is architectural: stop asking humans to copy truth that can be read directly. When a new repository appears in GitHub, detect it automatically. When a new workload runs in Kubernetes, index it. When OpenTelemetry traces show an undeclared dependency, surface it as drift.

When the catalog is derived from real infrastructure signals rather than maintained by humans, it stops drifting. Deviations from declared intent become first-class observable events rather than silent accumulation.

This is the principle behind Cartogra, the service intelligence platform I'm building in public. Every service in the registry was either auto-discovered via SCM scan or Kubernetes watch, or explicitly declared via API. Drift between declared and observed dependencies is surfaced in real time. Every state change is recorded as immutable history — so you can query what the dependency graph looked like at any point in the past.

The goal is a catalog that engineers trust because it earns that trust continuously, not one that asks engineers to maintain it.

If you manage platform infrastructure or are building internal developer tooling, I'd be curious: what does your current service catalog look like, and where does it break down?
