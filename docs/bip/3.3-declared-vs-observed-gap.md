## Blog Post (~850 words)

### Every service catalog is wrong by Tuesday

Someone fills in the catalog during a migration. Six weeks later `reporting-service` starts calling `payments-service` for a quick report, nobody updates the YAML, and the next incident review finds a dependency nobody knew about.

The usual fix is a process: quarterly reviews, PR templates with a "did you update the catalog?" checkbox. They get skipped, because writing the YAML doesn't help the person who has to write it.

### Two maps, not one

Cartogra keeps two sets of edges for every tenant:

- **Declared**: what a human said. Added in the UI, through the API, or by the seed loader.
- **Observed**: what the traffic shows. Derived from OpenTelemetry spans, with no human involved.

Neither one is the truth on its own. Declared edges go stale. Observed edges only cover calls that happened inside the observation window and were sampled. A nightly batch job that didn't run this week leaves no trace.

The interesting part is where they disagree, so Cartogra stores the disagreement as a first-class record: **drift**.

### Two kinds of drift

A sweep (every 5 minutes by default) compares the two sets per tenant, pair by pair (`source → target`, protocol ignored):

- **Undeclared**: observed, with no declared pair. Somebody added a call and didn't tell the catalog.
- **Missing**: declared more than `missing-after` ago (default 7 days) and no observed call since. Either the dependency is dead, or it's quiet and someone should know which.

Missing drift is only raised for tenants that send spans at all. Without that guard, a tenant with no tracing would see every declared edge flagged as missing on day 8.

A drift whose condition clears on its own (the call stops, or someone declares the edge) closes itself, with `resolution = AUTO`.

### Making it real: the Acme Fintech seed

Demos with three boxes and two arrows prove nothing, so Phase 3 shipped with a simulated customer: Acme Fintech. 21 services, 5 teams, 3 orphans, 2 cycles, one god service (`ledger-service`, 12 dependents). It runs as 21 Deployments of one generic fake-service image in a kind cluster, sending real OTel spans through a real collector.

Three calls in the seed are dormant: `reporting-service → payments-service`, `webhook-relay → fraud-service`, `legacy-batch → notification-service`. `pnpm acme drift on` wakes them up. Within one sweep, three undeclared drifts appear on the graph as solid amber edges. One declared call, `loyalty-service → fx-service`, is never made. Once the window passes it shows up dashed blue as missing.

Nobody typed those findings in. That's the point.

### What the UI does with it

The graph has four edge modes: All, Declared, Observed, Drift. Drift mode draws only the pairs that disagree. A legend tells you which line is which, and undeclared and missing also differ by dash pattern, not just colour.

Selecting a service and opening its Drift tab shows only the drifts that touch it. Engineers in an incident care about their service, not the whole tenant's to-do list.

Each drift has a one-step answer. Undeclared drift offers **Accept** (declare it). Missing drift offers **Remove declared**. Both offer **Dismiss**. That's the next post.

### The honest limits

- Spans only show what was sampled and what's instrumented. An uninstrumented proxy hides the call behind it.
- Pairs ignore protocol. If A calls B over HTTP and you declared gRPC, that's no drift. It's a deliberate simplification.
- Until trusted OTLP intake ships (roadmap 5.9), the tenant on a span is an unauthenticated attribute. That's fine for the dev stack and not for production, and ADR-0029 says so.

### Why this matters

A catalog that has to be maintained by hand is a catalog that dies. A catalog that can show you where it's wrong, with evidence from traffic, gets maintained as a side effect of looking at it.

## LinkedIn Post (~350 words)

Internal service catalogs die the same way: someone fills them in during a migration, and six weeks later they describe a system that no longer exists.

The fix is usually process — quarterly reviews, PR checkboxes. Those get skipped because the person updating the catalog gets nothing back for it.

In Cartogra we stopped treating the catalog as the source of truth. We keep two maps for every organisation: dependencies people *declared*, and dependencies we *observe* in OpenTelemetry traces. Neither is complete. Declared edges go stale; observed edges only see what was sampled.

The useful signal is where they disagree. We record that as "drift":

- **Undeclared** — traffic shows A calling B, nobody declared it.
- **Missing** — declared for over a week, no traffic since.

A background sweep raises drift, and closes it again when the condition clears. Missing drift only fires for organisations that send traces at all — otherwise anyone without tracing would see every edge flagged after a week.

To test this honestly we built a simulated customer: 21 services in a local Kubernetes cluster, emitting real spans. Switching on three dormant calls produces three drift records nobody typed in.

The trade-offs are written down: sampling hides calls, uninstrumented proxies hide more, and until authenticated ingestion ships, trace attribution is only safe for the dev environment.

A catalog that shows you where it's wrong, with evidence, gets maintained as a side effect of being used.
